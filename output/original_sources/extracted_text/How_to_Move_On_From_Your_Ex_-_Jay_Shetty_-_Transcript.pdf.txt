How to Move On From Your Ex — Jay Shetty
Source: If you still think about your ex every day and can't move on, please watch this — On Purpose with
Jay Shetty (YouTube)
Why you can't let go
Why can't you let go? When we fall in love, our brains release dopamine and oxytocin — the same
chemicals tied to addiction. So a breakup doesn't just hurt emotionally, it hurts physically: you're in
withdrawal from a person. But what really keeps you stuck isn't just the chemistry, it's identity. For
months or years you weren't just you — you were “us.” Your plans, routines, even your sense of
self were intertwined with theirs. When they leave, it feels like losing your reflection.
You chase closure, but what you really want is confirmation that you mattered. You replay every
conversation searching for the moment it could have gone differently, creating stories and meaning
that aren't backed by fact. That's not healing — it's your mind trying to rewrite a story it doesn't
want to end.
Here's the truth: we don't get addicted to people, we get addicted to how we felt around them —
wanted, seen, chosen. Now that they're gone, your brain is chasing that feeling, not the person. So
ask yourself: what part of me felt most alive in that relationship, and how can I give that back to
myself? Healing starts when you focus on the feeling, not the person — finding that connection
within yourself and with others. When you believe that person is more powerful than an experience
or emotion, you'll never let go. That emotion came through you and still exists within you.
Other people don't make us feel things — they push buttons, and we feel what's already inside us.
Wayne Dyer's example: squeeze an orange and you get orange juice, never lemon juice. What
comes out is what's already within. So whatever you loved experiencing with someone came from
within you and is still accessible.
The myths that keep you stuck
Myth 1 — Time heals everything. Time doesn't erase memories; it teaches you to carry them
differently. What matters is what you do with that time. Hooks like scrolling their social media,
looking at old pictures, and checking on them keep you imprisoned. Time can't help if you spend it
fixated on them.
Myth 2 — I just need closure. Even a perfect explanation wouldn't satisfy you; you'd find another
objection. The mind hates incompleteness and wants to complete the task, but completeness
rarely comes from someone else. What you're really saying is “I don't feel valued, I want you back.”
What worries you isn't what they think of you — it's that what they think might match what you fear
about yourself. Closure comes from growing and healing yourself.
Myth 3 — If I move on, it means I never cared. Something can be real for a season and not the
next. Trees have leaves and let them go in the fall — that doesn't make the leaves unreal. Moving
on means you learned what the relationship came to teach you. The Buddha: when you like a
flower you pluck it, but when you love a flower you water it every day. Real love shows up every
single day, not just on a wedding day or anniversary.
Myth 4 — If they came back, it would finally work. The promise to never complain or raise issues
again isn't love — is it love if you can't be honest or express your feelings without fear? You don't


miss them, you miss hope. But hope without change is just another heartbreak waiting to happen.
You broke up because of something deeply rooted; don't invite long-term drama back because that
person makes you feel peaceful short-term.
How to actually heal
1. Stop feeding the fantasy. You can't heal while romanticising the highlight reel. Block the
breadcrumbs — social media, old photos, playlists. Out of sight, out of mind. Look at both
storylines: write out every reason you weren't right for each other, not why they're a bad person.
Sometimes you're upset because you wanted to end things first but were scared. Fear keeps us in
the wrong relationships and pulls us back into them.
2. Feel without dramatizing. Grief is healthy; making it your identity isn't. Sit with the sadness, but
don't pitch a tent there. Journaling prompt: what did this relationship teach me about my needs, not
my worth? Reflecting onto your worth makes you feel worse; learning about your needs helps you
move forward.
3. Rebuild your rituals. Heartbreak steals structure; healing gives it back. Your brain had a rhythm
with that person — the morning text, the evening call, the weekend date. You had a rhythm before
them too. Create new anchors (walks, gym, therapy, dinners with friends), especially at the three
times of day you feel most triggered.
4. Shift the question. Instead of “why didn't it work,” ask “what version of me was I becoming while
trying to make it work?” The real reason it didn't work: they didn't want to make it work — and it
can't work just because you do. A healthy long-term relationship is two people committed to making
it work. There's no “right person,” only the person willing to show up and make things right.
5. Let pain become purpose. You're meant to evolve through your story, not erase it. Every
heartbreak can harden you or humanize you — the difference is whether you learn or linger. Like
kintsugi, the Japanese practice of repairing broken pottery with gold: the cracks become gold, and
the wounds prepare you for what you're looking for next.
What to do when you slip back
You will slip back — recovery is three steps forward, two steps back. You might feel strong for
weeks, then see their name and everything floods back. That doesn't mean you're broken; it means
you're human. Missing someone doesn't mean you're meant for them — it means they occupied a
meaningful chapter and your heart hasn't caught up to the ending.
When it happens: instead of texting them, text a friend. Instead of rereading old messages, create
new connections. Instead of asking “do they miss me?”, ask “am I proud of who I'm becoming?”
Getting over someone isn't about forgetting them — it's about remembering you. There was a you
before, during, and after this relationship, and that's the most important relationship you'll ever
commit to.
The hardest part often isn't letting go of them — it's letting go of the future you built with them in
your mind. Healing starts when you stop asking “why did they leave” and start asking “what is this
pain trying to teach me?” Breathe in acceptance, breathe out attachment. You're not weak for still
feeling; you're strong for still showing up. You don't have to get over them today — just focus on
getting back to you.
