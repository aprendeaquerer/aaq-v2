To Anyone Going Through a Breakup — Mel Robbins
Source: To Anyone Going Through a Breakup: How to Heal a Broken Heart & Move On — The Mel Robbins
Podcast (YouTube)
Introduction & the worst advice
Mel Robbins and her daughter Sawyer share a breakup survival guide built from Sawyer's
experience of the biggest heartbreak of her life and from research for the love-and-breakups
section of the Let Them Theory book. The premise: a breakup is as painful as grief, and on the
other side of it is a wiser, more loving version of you.
First, the worst advice: “love yourself.” After a breakup you often hate yourself — you feel insecure,
unwanted, rejected — and being told to love yourself when the person you wanted love from is
gone is useless. So is “get back out there” and “distract yourself,” because you think about them all
day anyway. Let people feel what they need to feel.
Processing: a breakup is grief in the nervous system
A key distinction: processing a breakup vs. supporting yourself to move through it and build a life
without the person. We collapse the two, which is why the advice fails. Sobbing on the floor isn't a
tool — it's processing. (And avoid the “revenge diet”: getting fit to get back at someone keeps you
attached to them. Prioritise yourself for you, not for them.)
Processing a breakup is a neurological, physiological, psychological and chemical process. In a
relationship you become intertwined with the other person, so you have to untangle and deprogram
having them in your day-to-day life. That's why people keep their ex front and center — stalking
Instagram, posting to be seen, replaying memories, bringing the ex up in every conversation —
because the patterns are wired in.
Sawyer's therapist (An Davin) explained it hurts because everything about them is intertwined in
your nervous system. You miss them, but your nervous system also misses them. Heartbreak is
the same as grief: the life you thought you'd live has died, and you'll move through the stages of
grief for days, weeks, even months. You have to unlearn your life with them so you can start living
your life without them — as you walk or drive you'll imagine them there, hear what they'd say, want
to share good news with them.
This reframe builds self-compassion: you're not a weak weirdo — your body is wired to do life with
this person, and now you're learning to not do life with them. It's the circuitry, the nervous system,
the thoughts, the songs.
The 30-day no-contact rule
The 30-day no-contact rule (from An Davin): no contact at all for 30 days — and especially no
videos or voice memos, because their voice is so supercharged in your nervous system that
hearing it reactivates the old patterns and forces a big step back. Mel even hid the couple's photos
from the family digital frame to help.
Sawyer's history: in her first heartbreak she reached out immediately, they got back together, and it
set up five years of cycling in and out of each other's lives. This time she had 25 impulses a day to
reach out, but the rule gave her something to work toward. She waited the full 30 days. On day 30


she called — and could later see it probably set her back and didn't give the “closure” she wanted.
On closure: no one else can give it to you. People seek closure because they don't understand
they're grieving and deprogramming a shared life — they think one conversation will reset their
nervous system, but that work is internal. Wanting to reach out, text their parents, or send a meme
is normal withdrawal and habit — not a sign from God that they're your person. You give space and
compassion to the impulse without acting on it: let them leave, and let me keep my promise of no
contact.
The timeline: 3 months, and the 11-week mark
Expect at least three months in the thick of it — that's how long research says it takes to grieve a
breakup before you start to feel better. By around the 11-week mark, about 71% of people feel
better. Having that mile marker is comforting. The sadness is a mentally healthy response to
heartbreak.
Use “let them”: let them date other people, let them not reach out, let them look like their life's a
party — paired with “let me”: let me keep my no-contact promise, let me grieve, let me be sad.
Sawyer found the only thing that finally brought acceptance was admitting the future she'd built in
her mind was never going to happen — that he wouldn't be at the end of the aisle. Clinging to the
sadness was an anchor that kept her tied to him, just as some people hold onto anger to stay
connected to an ex.
After a breakup we replay only the good memories and block out the dysfunction, making it seem
perfect — which feeds the fantasy. The inability to accept reality and learn a new way through life is
what keeps you trapped.
Six things that help you process
1. Remove the triggers. Take down photos, pause the old playlists, box up their clothing and
trinkets and put them away (don't burn them). If you can't, ask a parent or roommate to be more
ruthless than you'd be. Any physical reminder fires up your nervous system.
2. Give your bedroom a makeover. Paint a wall, move the bed, buy new sheets — signal a new
chapter, because your bedroom is “ground zero” for the sadness. If you're supporting someone,
help create that change (buy them a new blanket instead of covering a bar tab).
3. Reach out and ask for help. There's a hole to fill, so proactively tell roommates, siblings,
co-workers and parents what's happening and ask them to check in and invite you out for the next
three months. The act of going out is itself a sign you're moving on.
4. Fill your calendar. Search for events near you and ask friends along. Without things to look
forward to you'll feel like a depressed loser; with a calendar you step back into life. Fill it with things
that bring joy, not work or obligation.
5. Pick a challenge you've always wanted to do. Climb a mountain, train for a triathlon, learn guitar,
take a trip — something you didn't have time for in the relationship. It gives you purpose beyond
the person you're getting over.
6. Ask the reframing question. “If you knew the love of your life was around the corner and this
breakup was bringing you one step closer to meeting them, how would you spend your nights and
weekends while you're single?” It pulls you out of fear of being single and back into living.


Listener questions: what-ifs, dating again, the spark, living for your ex
On the “what ifs”: when you say “what if,” you're not on a date with one person — you're in a
“thruple” with the fantasy. Replace “what if I never find someone” with “what if this works out / leads
me closer to what I deserve.” The purpose of dating right now isn't to find the one, it's to find
yourself. Use the 80/20 rule: focus on the 80% that matters (are they kind, do they make you laugh,
do you feel like yourself, do you share goals) and ignore the 20% of fantasy and fear.
On dating again after a bad breakup: if you date to get over someone, you won't find the right
person — and you may not be interested in dating until you've truly processed and dropped the
fantasy. It's okay not to date. You don't have to be actively dating to meet someone; being out in
the world as yourself, open and happy, is often when the right person shows up.
On the spark: only about 11% of relationships begin with a spark — most are slow burns (the friend
of a friend, the person down the hall). A spark is attraction; love is compatibility, commitment,
kindness — specifically consideration (keeping someone in mind, the small acts) and admiration
(seeing attributes you respect), which grow over time. Disney trained us to chase the spark; the
real world works on admiration and consideration.
On living your life for your ex (looking good, posting, dating to make them jealous): your life won't
begin until you let them live theirs. Navigating life by how they'd react keeps you hostage to a
relationship that's over. Go back to the 30 days, unfollow, remove reminders, change your routines,
and truly grieve — only then do you take your power back.
Closing
Closing: you can survive this, and it does get better. Every ending is also a beginning. A breakup is
teaching your body how to go through life without someone who used to be in it — it's grief, new
patterns, and a period of withdrawal, and all those emotions are normal (they don't mean this was
your person). Do whatever you need to process it: be sad, cry, eat your way through three months
if you must.
You deserve love simply because you're alive — not because someone else gives you a reason.
The fantasy in your mind is what's killing you; let them go, and let me step into reality. Use the
tools, and in a few months you'll look up and realise you're going to be okay. The greatest love of
your life may be on the road ahead.
