Leadership, control, and emotions as data
Most men, Connor Beaton included, were taught that leadership is about control — of others, of
outcomes, and most dangerously of emotions. In his book Men's Work he writes about how men
are taught that psychological and emotional strength comes through suppression: stuff it down,
deal with it, “big boys don't cry.” Many male-leadership examples appear disconnected and almost
dissociated from emotion.
That isn't entirely wrong — we don't want emotions to nuke our lives or fully control us. The
problem is the vehicle: suppression, numbing, and ignoring. Instead, view emotions as just another
data set, like thoughts or senses. They shouldn't be the only source you make decisions by, but
they're part of the equation in love, sex, business, and relationships. Pretending you can ignore
your emotions and still get the outcomes you want almost never works.
The myth of the stoic leader
The myth of the stoic leader: that emotions equal weakness, so you box them away, and the more
you suppress, the more masculine and capable you become. That's not strength — it's
suppression. As Jung said, until you make the unconscious conscious, it will direct your life and
you'll call it fate. Suppressed emotions don't vanish; they become a force pushing you in different
directions.
Emotional intelligence isn't about being soft — it's about knowing what's happening inside you
before it hijacks your behaviour. People misread the Stoics: Marcus Aurelius may have been one
of the most emotionally intelligent men ever, with extreme self-reflection. When you can name
what's happening inside you, you can navigate it; when you can't, it controls you.
There are moments — the author has worked with military and former Navy SEALs — where
feelings genuinely need to be shelved so you can take definitive action. But suppression as your
primary way of dealing with emotion is defective; you won't function well in relationships, with your
kids, partner, boss, friends, or family.
The four traps
Trap 1 — The stoic mask: believing composure means emotionlessness. These men exert high
control over themselves and others, lead from intellect only, and come across as cold and
disconnected (“I can't feel you”).
Trap 2 — The emotional flutter: men who open up but the floodgates pour out. They vent instead of
express (Dr. Glover calls it “emotional puking”), mistaking catharsis for connection. Emotions spill
over instead of being understood, contained, and integrated.
Trap 3 — The outsourcer (the “nice guy”): expecting others — usually the women they date — to
do their emotional labour. Often unconscious: “tell me what's wrong with me,” “help me calm
down.” Emotional intelligence is self-generated, not outsourced. Co-regulation and support are
fine, but the outsourcer hands his internal data to someone else to interpret instead of reading it


himself.
Trap 4 — The avoidant achiever: hiding in work, money, and status to avoid feeling, believing
leadership is purely about achievement, not presence or relationality. True leadership is embodied,
not performed.
What emotional intelligence actually is
Emotional intelligence (EQ) isn't about being overly emotional — it's about being aware of and
accountable for what you feel, and translating that awareness into wise action. Daniel Goleman
breaks EQ into four parts:
Self-awareness — knowing what you're feeling and why. Self-regulation — managing emotions
before they manage you (not locking them down, but keeping them from consuming you). Empathy
— understanding what others feel without losing your own centre (neither the disconnected feeler
who lacks empathy, nor the man so overly empathetic that he gets consumed by his partner's
feelings and can't actually support her). Relationship management — navigating conflict, building
trust, and leading through connection rather than manipulation or coercion.
Couple these four with assertiveness and direction and you have a very potent, competent, and
attractive man.
Containment and the 3A model
The key difference to remember: healthy masculine leadership contains emotion — it doesn't
collapse into it or cut itself off from it. It's like a riverbank giving the water direction: without the
banks, emotions flood everywhere; without the water, there's no life. Containment without
suppression, expression without chaos. As Seneca wrote, “he who conquers himself is the
mightiest warrior” — meaning deep self-awareness, not killing off emotion.
The 3A model of emotional intelligence:
A1 — Awareness: name it to tame it. When you feel off, pause and ask: what am I actually feeling,
where do I feel it in my body, and what story is my mind telling me? (As Frankl noted, between
stimulus and response there is a pause.) For example: “I feel protective because I sense
disrespect,” vs. “I feel protective because I'm not getting what I want.” That distinction changes
your reaction entirely.
A2 — Alchemize: regulate before you respond. Create space; never lead, speak, or decide when
dysregulated — it's the most disempowered position to lead from. It's fine to say “let me sit with that
and get back to you tomorrow.” Masculine regulation is physical and others can feel it; once your
nervous system settles, clarity follows (a stressed brain is prioritising survival, not good decisions).
A3 — Action: lead through presence. Once you can respond rather than react, speak directly,
truthfully, calmly, and clearly, and take the risk to act. Action without awareness is reckless;
awareness without action is just avoidance — and many men get stuck with all the awareness but
take no action.
The call to masculine leadership
The call: the world doesn't need more emotionally numb men, nor more emotionally reactive men.
It needs men who can hold and contain complexity, intensity, and charge — men who feel deeply,


think clearly, and act decisively. The two traits worth developing most (in ourselves and the next
generation) are adaptability and the ability to regulate and calm the nervous system under
pressure, because it's nearly impossible to lead well from a place of dysregulation.
As David Deida said, “the masculine grows by challenge.” So here's the challenge: don't avoid
what you feel, stop using suppression as leadership — face it, master it, and let it forge you into
someone trustworthy and potent.
