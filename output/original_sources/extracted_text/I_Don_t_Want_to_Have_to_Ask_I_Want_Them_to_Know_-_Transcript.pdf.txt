I Don't Want to Have to Ask — I Want Them to Know
The desire underneath “they should just know”
“I don't want to have to ask, I want them to know.” This is such a common experience, and I feel it
deeply when women bring it to me, because underneath that statement is a very deep desire to be
known, to be seen, to be understood, to be considered.
This is not about lowering your standards or asking for the bare minimum. If a person can't offer
you the bare minimum and you have to ask for basic human decency, then we're dating the wrong
person. This is about learning to take up space so we can get what we're wanting.
The thought “they should just know” is rarely entitlement — it's a deep need for attunement, to be
seen and considered, especially if this is something we didn't really get in childhood. Instead we
may have been made to feel that our needs and desires are a burden, too much, somehow too
heavy for someone else to carry.
So we carry a void around being deeply seen and accepted in our desires and needs. And what
fills that void more than a person being so accepting of your needs and desires that they anticipate
them?
How we adapt, and how needs come out sideways
On top of that, if we've had experiences where our needs were not okay and our desires were too
big, we develop a fear of expressing or showing those parts of ourselves, because we experienced
rejection and shaming — so we adapt. “If I get hurt when I say these things, then I might just not
say them anymore.”
But just because we don't speak our desires and needs out loud doesn't mean they go away. They
stay there, unfulfilled — and when our needs aren't met and our desires aren't fulfilled, we feel
discomfort, and those needs come out sideways.
This is where we develop unspoken expectations: we project our understanding of how we want to
be loved, and if the other person doesn't behave in those expected ways, we conclude that they
don't love us, don't like us, or aren't a good person. We project our disowned needs onto the world
around us because we're not owning them.
Or they come out as passive aggression and resentment — and you may not even know why,
because you might not be aware of the exact needs and desires that haven't been met. You just
notice resentment, or being more annoyed than usual with your partner, because there's more
happening under the surface that hasn't been put on the table.
The price of nourishing love is vulnerability
Here's one really significant thing: the price for being loved really well — in a way that feels good,
nourishing, and supportive — is vulnerability. A great relationship is based on vulnerability,
connection, and intimacy: I let you in, I let you see me, even though that's scary, even though it's a
risk.
If we're unwilling to let someone see us because we're scared — which is valid and
understandable — but we don't let them know what makes us happy, how we like to be loved,


what's important to us, what makes us feel loved, then we likely won't get what we want.
We've been sold a fairy-tale idea of love: once we're together it's happily ever after, everything is
perfect, the partner is perfect, life is perfect. That's not real. Love has a price, and the price is
vulnerability — you need to let them in.
So we need to work on the shame we hold around our needs, our pain, and our desires. This is
where the work is: learning to experience the fear and discomfort of leaning into our edge and
trying something new. Your voice is power — you need to use it.
Asking is an invitation, not begging
“But I don't want to beg.” That's the shame around having needs and desires coming up — we
easily experience ourselves as “less than” when asking for something. But when you communicate
a desire or need from a grounded place, it's not begging — it's an invitation: “This is how I like to be
related to, this is what makes me feel loved, this is what I like.”
So you tell a partner or someone you're seeing: “Valentine's Day is important to me,” or “I like to be
surprised on dates.” Just make sure the desire is actually yours, not something you're making up.
Asking for what you want is an invitation: “You want to be in a relationship with me? This is what I
like. I'll show you how I want to be related to — now show me who you are.”
That's where we give them space to respond, and that's where we often lose our heads and miss
red flags. If you never ask because “I just want them to know,” you never get to see how they
respond when you make yourself vulnerable and show a deeper layer of yourself. It doesn't have to
be your deepest trauma — it can be “I like when men take the lead in dating, initiate and plan
dates.” That moment shows you who you're with.
Ask for what you want. You don't need a partner who reads your mind and is always perfect —
perfect doesn't exist. What you need is a partner who cares, so when you say “babe, I want more
of this” or “this would make me happy,” they listen and think: “I want to make her feel that way,
safe, loved.” But you'll never know if you have a partner like this unless you put it to the test —
unless you ask, make yourself vulnerable, and see what they do with it.
This doesn't mean they always have to say yes. But you get to see: are they kind? are they caring?
are they considerate? A man who responds to your desire or need with dismissal — “you're too
sensitive,” “you're too much,” “you're always asking for so many things” — is someone to run away
from. We're communicating preferences, desires, needs; now show me who you are.
Your voice is your power
When you stay silent, you have no power in that scenario, so of course it doesn't feel great. Your
voice is your power. If you're scared of using it, that's probably because at some point someone
told you to shut up — and that's something we want to undo. There's nothing shameful about your
desires and needs; they're human.
The right person wants to hear about your needs and desires; the right person wants to love you
well. Don't take away your own chance of being seen if you're with a person like that. Ask: “What
can I do to love you better? What would you like to receive more of?” Have these conversations
even in long relationships.


Remember: relationships die because of silence. We need to communicate, we need to be intimate
— that's what creates closeness.
