Why You're Struggling to Find Love (and how to
 change it)
Source: Why You're Struggling to Find Love (and how to change it) — YouTube
Introduction
What if you've been approaching dating all wrong? Maybe you've put yourself out there, but
nothing clicks, or you're still waiting for the right person. Over half of singles today are open to love,
yet more than 60% feel burned out by apps. The truth is, finding love isn't about perfection. It's
about showing up authentically, reading the right signals, and attracting real connection.
In this episode, host Jay Shetty is joined by body language specialist Vanessa Van Edwards, love
expert Jillian Turecki, relationship coach Sadia Khan, and psychotherapist Lori Gottlieb — a
masterclass on attraction, chemistry, and building relationships that last.
Vanessa Van Edwards — Body language & availability
Vanessa Van Edwards shifts our attention to the subtleties of connection, specifically the cues we
give off in dating and attraction that often go unnoticed. We think we're obvious when we're
interested, but research shows we're not. People recognise flirting only 28% of the time, and
women often have to send multiple signals quickly just to be understood.
It's a phenomenon called signal amplification bias: we tend to think we are over-obvious with our
cues. They studied singles in a bar/nightclub setting and counted each person's flirtatious signals.
It took 29 signals in 10 minutes for a woman to get approached — for the other person to register
“oh, she's interested.” That's before the conversation even started. Most women think three
glances should be enough.
In the same studies they found that the most attractive women who didn't signal enough got
approached less than less-attractive women who did signal availability. So availability actually
makes you more attractive. Your hair, outfit, how you look and smell are great, but they won't work
if you don't know the body language signals of availability — and you have to be super clear with
them.
The signals: Flirty glances. We sweep the room with our eyes, see someone we like, and give a
brief side glance, or a down-and-up look (looking up through the lashes — think Marilyn Monroe).
Then eye contact, look away, and little smiles. Remember it took 29 of these, so get comfortable
with trying — and there's no pressure if he doesn't return it.
Self-touch is also a flirty signal: playing with your hair (signalling hair health), or touching your neck,
lips, or chin — which actually releases pheromones and scent. Scent matters: in one study, women
wore plain white t-shirts for 24 hours; the smell other women liked best predicted who they liked
best in person. So self-touch is partly about releasing that natural scent.
The best way to show availability is one word: “Hey.” Low pressure, for both men and women. If
you walk by someone — even with AirPods in — and say “hey,” if they like you they'll say “hey”
back. Tone matters: use the lowest end of your natural voice. We decide how confident someone is
within the first 200 milliseconds of hearing them speak. We listen for relaxation and breath in the
vocal cords, and we subconsciously match the voice resonance of the most important person in the
room.


Key takeaway: confidence and availability beat attractiveness every time. People respond to
signals of openness, not perfection. Flirty glances, leaning in, and mirroring someone's energy
make a huge difference — it just requires intentionality and lowering the stakes, so that even a
casual “hey” can lead to big results.
Jillian Turecki — Dating mistakes & slowing down
Jillian Turecki shares the biggest dating mistakes people make. Mistake one: relying only on the
apps. If you just focus on that, it burns you out. You have to be proactive, widen your circle, and
also let things unfold organically. Mistake two: impatience — it's not every day you meet someone
you want to build a relationship with. Mistake three: high expectations. Go on dates with as low an
expectation as possible, and treat dating as a chance to practice your social skills.
A bonus mistake: long weeks-long texting exchanges that give a false sense of intimacy. Text a
little back and forth for a day, then make a date — FaceTime, Zoom, or in person. Your time is too
precious to waste texting a stranger who may not even be available.
At the root of this is fear of rejection — and there are two kinds: the person doesn't want you, and
the person doesn't meet your expectations (you don't want them). The quality of our lives is very
much determined by how well we can confront rejection. If someone isn't into you in early dating,
they are absolutely not for you. Build the resilience-against-rejection muscle; it makes you more
attractive and it's just part of life.
You're looking for one person to fall in love with and for them to fall in love with you — and to find
that one, you'll have to meet a lot of people. All you need is one person to say yes. But by the
nature of odds, that probably won't be the next person.
Go in at the lowest baseline expectation — that lets it become a friendship, a “nothing-ship,”
whatever it actually is — instead of walking in deciding “this is going to be my wife/husband.” We're
addicted to speeding up love. How do we slow it down? Number one: there is no “the one.” We
choose who the one is. Love is a feeling, but it's also a choice — one you make many times
throughout a relationship: “I choose you.”
When there's chemistry, slow down and process your enthusiasm. Acknowledge the feeling — “this
feels good, this is exciting” — but then uncover the person's character and values, and get clearer
on what you need (not just prefer) to function in a relationship. Who you decide to partner with is
one of the most important decisions of your life, and we're somewhat meant to get it wrong along
the way. If you rush it, you're doing yourself a disservice.
There's the pain of being single and the pain of being in the wrong relationship — and the pain of
being single is a lot better. When you're single, the promise of a relationship is so alluring and
intoxicating that we feel we must have it now. Many who stay in wrong relationships do so because
they fear the unknown.
Key takeaway: while you're only looking for that one person, it might not be the next person — but
don't stop showing up. Use discernment to get clearer, stronger, and more grounded in who you
are and what you need.
Sadia Khan — What you entertain, not what you attract
Sadia Khan reframes attraction. Nearly 80% of daters report being ghosted at least once. Attraction
isn't the issue — most of us can attract all types of people. The problem is what we normalise: the


late-night text, the dismissiveness, the person who never makes space for us. “It's not what we
attract, it's what we entertain.”
With low self-esteem, we hold personal prophecies that people are supposed to be a bit
unavailable, that it's normal he doesn't text back or only texts at 12am on a Friday. The moment
you figure out not what you're attracting but what you're entertaining, you take your power back: “I
attract all types, but the ones I keep forming attachments to are the ones that leave me anxious.”
That anxiety is a signal of commitment issues, not chemistry.
Why are we more attracted to unavailable people? Low self-esteem makes us feel we don't
deserve to be adored. And unavailable men project the idea of having options; their mystery makes
them seem desirable. But when you realise it's actually emotional immaturity — he doesn't know
how to commit or focus on one person — they stop being attractive. Nobody is too busy to send
one text.
On apps, check whether you're really not matching, or just disliking who you match with. Social
media makes us compare real people to an algorithm of “high value” ideals. The more you like
yourself, the more you like people who like you. Start with the pool of people who actually like you,
then look for similar demographics and values — rather than glorifying people you can't access.
“Why do I have to settle?” If similarity feels like settling, maybe your standards are too high or
inflated. If what you bring and what you ask for are matched, you're not asking for too much — but
if you're asking people to fill the gaps in your self-worth, you may need to adapt your standards.
On ghosting: keep reasonable expectations. In the digital world people see each other as
disposable and feel they owe no explanation; ghosters are often buying time to heal or have met
alternatives. Don't take it too personally — but regardless of the reason, ghosters are poor
communicators. Reduce your attraction to people who can't communicate well.
Key takeaway: it's not about attracting the right person, but having the self-esteem to stop
entertaining the wrong ones. If someone leaves you anxious, that's immaturity, not chemistry. Build
your self-esteem, use your agency, and invest in those who truly value you.
Lori Gottlieb — Honesty, presence & repair
Lori Gottlieb shows how to face the hardest moments in dating: speaking your truth, knowing when
to stay, and having the courage to leave. Being honest about what you want isn't needy — it's
brave. About 60% of people admit staying in relationships longer than they should, while only 6%
leave too soon. Avoiding honesty isn't kindness, it's avoidance.
If you'd rather be exclusive, just be honest: “I can't really get close to you if you're dating other
people right now. Are you ready to be in an exclusive relationship?” They can say yes or no, and
either way you've learned a lot. The whole game is bringing your true self to the relationship. If you
can't practice that at any point, you're not ready for the long term — you can't wait until you're
engaged to suddenly bring your true self.
A common trap is “future tripping”: planning a future in your head — imagining the wedding, the
kids, “this could be it” — when things look good, then getting a reality check when the other person
doesn't mirror it back. The healthy version of future tripping is being in the present: what's
happening now is what the future will look like.


Instead of imagining the person will change, look at how they treat you now. The biggest indicator
is conflict: how did you get through a disagreement? That's what your future looks like. We talk
about rupture and repair — everyone has ruptures. If you've been dating six months with no
rupture, you're not going deep enough; you're still on best behaviour.
A good repair sounds like: “Let's take 15 minutes and come back when we're not so heated,” or “I
thought about it, I was wrong, I'm sorry, here's what I'd do differently.” And a partner who can
receive that without shaming you — “I appreciate that, and how can I be more supportive next
time?” — that's beautiful. That's your future, but you have to see it in the present. If you're making
plans in your head but are uncomfortable talking about them, they only exist in your head — they
aren't real.
Closing
Closing takeaways: You can't attract the right person until you get to know yourself. You can't
receive what you want until you learn to ask for it. You will attract what you're willing to entertain.
And real chemistry comes from honesty, openness, and courage. Love isn't found, it's created —
and you're worthy of creating it.
