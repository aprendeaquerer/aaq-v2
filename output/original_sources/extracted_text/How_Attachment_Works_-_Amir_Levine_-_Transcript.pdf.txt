How Attachment Works, with Amir Levine
Source: How Attachment Works, With Amir Levine — YouTube
Why attachment gets a bad rap
Amir Levine: I hope attachment gets less of a bad rap now than when I wrote the book. One of my
missions was to explain that the whole idea that we have to be independent and rely only on
ourselves just doesn't coalesce with our biology. It's a nice idea to think about not depending on
other people, but we're not built that way. In fact, we're built to be very intricately involved with
other people. We need other people, they need us, and we hardly do anything alone — even when
we're sitting and thinking, it's often a conversation.
Especially for humans, the default network in the brain is all about relationships. We're not a
particularly strong species physically, yet we can inhabit almost any place on the planet, including
harsh climates, partly because we collaborate and protect ourselves together.
Connection as emotional regulation
Sometimes I tell patients to text me in real time during treatment, because I'm also a child
psychiatrist and I do what we call secure priming: when they get upset with each other, a sentence
or two can revert them to a more secure stance, and they do much better. One girlfriend asked,
“Why do you have to text him, can't you just deal with it on your own?” But that's such a myth — we
never deal with things entirely on our own.
One of the most powerful ways of regulating emotional distress is forming a connection with
someone we're securely attached to — more powerful than any Xanax or pill, just a few minutes of
reaching out. But at the same time, one of the most powerful generators of emotional distress is
insecure relationships. So attachment is the basis of both suffering and healing from suffering.
That's why it's so important.
Attachment styles: effective vs. ineffective
About 55% of people are securely attached, and the rest are split between anxious and avoidant
styles (with a small anxious-avoidant group). What I love about this field is that it doesn't come from
the medical model — it takes a developmental stance. You don't think in terms of healthy or sick,
but whether a behaviour is working for you or not, effective or ineffective.
If you give people a chance to learn what's more effective, it's a powerful experience. You can
teach a small yet super effective way of interacting with a partner, and it takes them on a different
trajectory — they see it really works, and they take it further and further. People can become more
secure over time given the right surroundings.
Example: a patient resented that his girlfriend wouldn't get a certain treatment. I reminded him that
it took him almost a year to follow one of my own recommendations — so why expect her to listen
immediately? He got it, and told her: “I understand why you wouldn't want to do all these things. I
had a hard time following recommendations too. I want to be there for you and help, but I don't
want to force you.” That changed everything: she felt understood and heard, which is the heart of
secure attachment.
Where attachment styles come from


Where do attachment styles come from? Some say it stems from childhood, and partly it may. But I
personally don't think it's completely known. Some may come from early childhood, some from
later experiences — even adolescence, when someone breaks your heart and unsettles your faith
in people.
Stepping back to an evolutionary perspective, there's an advantage to heterogeneity in the
population around attitudes toward closeness and danger. It comes down to two things: how much
you want intimacy and closeness, and how sensitive your radar is to potential threats to that
closeness.
If you love intimacy but have a very sensitive threat radar, you're anxious. If you love intimacy but
threats go over your head, you're secure. If too much closeness makes you uneasy so you push
the person away, you're avoidant. It's simple.
There's an advantage to having some hypervigilant people. During the pandemic, spouses differed
on how much to hunker down, mask, and stay home — and a very vigilant partner you comply with
can be the difference between life and death. Sometimes a high-alert system helps, sometimes it
doesn't, so evolution favours a mix.
You see this throughout biology. Even in C. elegans, a tiny worm, there are two feeding
behaviours: aggregate feeders that swarm to food, and solitary feeders that move away — and a
single gene mutation can switch between them. Aggregate feeding is great when there's lots of
food (“come have a feast”), but it's also perfect for a predator; solitary feeding protects against
being eaten. It's a basic principle running through biology — so it's not only about how we were
treated as children; it goes much deeper.
How the danger radar is calibrated
This doesn't mean a mother or caretaker bears sole responsibility for whether a child can attach.
Children's attachment styles even differ between different caregivers — we have a much richer
repertoire than people think. But there's some baseline: some people identify danger more easily
than others. It's not that one is better; it's how the radar is calibrated for danger. That's why the
same person can be securely attached to one person and anxious or avoidant with someone else.
Writing the book, I learned to fall in love with secure people. Before, I took them for granted.
They're the ones who always call when they say they'll call, always show up on time, always reply.
Things go easy with them; you know you're always welcome. We all have some of these people.
But our social brain hates exclusion and loves hyper-inclusion, so our attention drifts toward where
there's strife — who didn't call, who excluded us. Part of what I teach is to shift your gaze and focus
more on the secure people: turn down the volume on insecurity, turn up the volume on security.
Secure people simply don't see danger as much, which makes life easier — as long as the
environment truly isn't dangerous. One patient woke her husband at 6am in 2008 with a bad feeling
and wouldn't let it go; he liquidated all their stock, and about a month later the market crashed.
Research shows people with an anxious working model are more sensitive at reading facial
expressions of anger or happiness. But when the environment is safe, it's nicer to be around
someone secure who loves closeness and doesn't get worked up over little things.
Micro needs and the secure radar


When you're with someone secure, there's nothing to trip the radar system. Researchers looked for
the secret of secure mothers — do they hold their children more, spend more time, are they more
in tune? It turned out they simply have a sixth sense of when their child needs help. Secure
spouses are the same: they have a knack for what the other person needs. I call it theory of mind
— putting yourself in someone else's shoes — and they know it's their responsibility to take care of
those needs.
I should really call them micro needs, because the system isn't about big needs — it's about small
things. The radar doesn't care about huge gestures; it cares about the small things in life. This
radar exists in all our relationships: parent-child, adult romantic, friendships, and the workplace.
Some bosses are amazingly secure, some are not.
I once worked with a very anxious colleague. At first, when she called ten times, I'd ignore her —
which made it worse. Then I learned to preemptively call her, attune to what she might need, and
everything flowed much more smoothly. We ended up working really well together.
Inclusion and belonging
Different relationships have different structures, but certain principles cut through all of them —
especially the need to feel included and to belong. Imagine you've worked on a project for two
years and find out your boss held a meeting and didn't invite you. An anxious person thinks: he's
trying to exclude me, take the credit, take it away from me. But in a secure relationship you might
think: he knows how much I hate these meetings, he values my time, he knows I don't need to be
there. It's a completely different way of looking at the world. Whether something feels dangerous or
safe cuts through many relationships.
Effective relationships and the safety mechanism
I don't look at relationships as healthy or unhealthy, but effective or ineffective. My two mentors —
a Nobel laureate and his wife, both professors — were completely codependent: she'd call him
every two hours, they came and left together, she managed his schedule. But it truly worked for
them; they were a high-achieving power couple. So I ask: is it working for you or not?
Relationships and feeling connected are a safety mechanism — that's how humans feel safe. We
don't feel safe because of money in the bank or an expensive car; when the system was formed we
didn't have those things. We feel safe through other people (and so do social animals, like dogs).
Effective relationships are ones you don't think much about, because they're there and you feel
safe. That safety kicks in the exploratory drive: you look out into the world, you create, you feel
someone has your back, so you can be braver and try new things. That's the function of effective
relationships, whether romantic, friendship, or workplace — people achieve a lot.
We're taught to train ourselves to have a healthy relationship as if we know what that is before we
know who it's with. But it's more about finding someone who can see you and meet your micro
needs even before you ask. That takes talent — secure people have it, and it's underappreciated
because they do it seamlessly. In psychiatry we often don't see secure people because they don't
come to therapy; they're busy having good, happy lives. But those relationships are very common.
If you take a patient's therapy hours and teach them to transform their social milieu into a more
secure one, you set them up for success — they go out and have secure people around them who
really listen. For patients used only to insecure interactions, secure behaviour first feels like scary,


unfamiliar terrain (“are you sure danger isn't coming?”). With reassurance, after a while they realise
there isn't danger, and it actually feels comfortable.
The myth of loving yourself first
Another counterintuitive point: the cliché that you must love yourself before you can love someone
else. I don't go along with that. In high school, my co-author Rachel and I had a friend with terrible
self-esteem, eating disorders, depression and anxiety, who really did not like herself. Then she met
someone who simply adored her and worshipped the ground she walked on — and over time you
could see her transform. So I came to believe in the transformative power of love.
The most powerful way of regulating emotional distress is proximity to someone we're securely
attached to. We're a social species; our brain is built for that. We need that message in a “me, me,
me” culture that's so solipsistic and self-absorbed — it just isn't our biology.
And how do you even learn to love yourself? You go to therapy — but that's working with someone
else. You talk to a friend. How do you learn to love yourself entirely on your own? Maybe you
meditate, fine, but eventually you have to interact with someone. The trick is to find the right people
to give you secure love, so you can prosper, evolve, and give love back.
How little the system needs to feel safe
This system actually doesn't need a lot to feel safe — that's what most people, especially avoidant
people, don't understand. It's scary to them: “I'm going to drown in that need.” But it's like
parenting: you're better off taking care of a need before it becomes huge. Put a child to sleep
before they're overtired and cranky; feed them before they're starving. Identify things early and take
care of them before they get out of control.
Once we form an attachment, our autonomic nervous system becomes dependent on the other
person. The quality of a relationship can affect blood pressure, breathing, heart rate, sleep, even
the immune system — things we don't control. The idea that we all stay independent and don't care
what the other does just doesn't hold up.
A COVID example: a couple with four young kids — endless work — used to handling it
themselves. They went to stay with the father-in-law in a big rural house. Now they do the same
work, but he sits on the couch watching TV while they run around, and they develop huge
resentment: “He's not helping us.” Insert another person into the equation and the whole
perspective changes. You can learn to love yourself all you want, but the moment someone is
there and ignores that you're struggling, physiologically it becomes something different.
Apart vs. alone, and the value of solitude
There's a difference between being apart and being alone. When we attach to someone, our whole
physiology changes because it's a safety system: neurotransmitters and hormones shift their
expression. The stress signal that culminates in cortisol actually goes up, but the rest of the
cascade doesn't get triggered — so it's like a chemically triggered gun, much more sensitive to
separation. (This was found in animal models, but these things are likely conserved through
evolution.) The minute you separate an attached pair, that cascade fires. So being alone and being
apart are completely distinct for the brain.


Is solitude important in intimacy? Yes — when you have a secure base and know the other person
is attuned and there for you, you'll naturally be alone a lot: “I want to read this book, listen to this
podcast, exercise.” You don't think about the relationship much. Sometimes you invite the other
person; some couples do everything together, and that's fine if it's effective. The baseline is that
both people feel heard and understood.
Final advice
One piece of advice: the biggest takeaway from the book is to really appreciate the secure people
in your life, give them more attention, and foster those relationships. Turn up the volume on secure
relationships — those small interactions with secure people can be truly transformative to our
brain.
On trusting your feelings: it is and it isn't. My next book aims to give a blueprint for which feelings to
trust and which not to. It takes time to attach, but when we meet someone we're into romantically,
there's a big initial spark — and you need to know whether that spark will lead to a good match.
Some things you can trust; others you need to test before you trust them. Feelings run amok can
ruin a connection.
What people don't understand is our function in a relationship: to regulate the other person's
emotions, and vice versa — I'm this other person's Xanax. When a relationship is full of difficult,
hurtful feelings, something is off. Secure relationships are mostly smooth sailing, with difficult
moments here and there — not constant difficult emotions. If that happens, the bond's regulating
function is misaligned and needs to be realigned.
