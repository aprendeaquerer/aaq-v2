Balancing Compassion and Boundaries
Why we treat them as mutually exclusive
Let's look at the balance between compassion and boundaries in relationships. This is a hurdle if
we didn't experience secure attachment in childhood — we tend to treat the two as mutually
exclusive, as if we can only have one of them. It often correlates with our attachment style: on the
anxious end we drown in compassion and have few boundaries; on the avoidant end, the opposite.
But we need both to make relationships work. We need compassion, empathy, and the curiosity to
understand another person and what might be happening behind their actions — and at the same
time we need to draw very clear boundaries and follow through on them. Otherwise we end up
experiencing things we don't want to experience in relationships.
Drowning in compassion (the anxious end)
From a societal perspective, women especially are socialised into the overly compassionate end of
the spectrum — overly understanding, with the focus not on the impact other people's behaviour
has on us, but on where they might be coming from, what their intention was, what their
background is, so we can accommodate their experience. Our focus is outside of us, without really
considering how we're doing in the dynamic.
So when someone does (or doesn't do) something and we're in pain about it — they're
inconsistent, not caring, always defensive, not showing up the way we want — our awareness
immediately goes to over-analysing them: where might they be coming from? We spend a lot of
time talking about their behaviour and background, trying to figure out what's going on over there,
which distracts us from our own discomfort.
You may notice this when dating: someone isn't showing up, and instead of noticing “this doesn't
work for me, I don't feel appreciated in this connection,” we go “oh, he's avoidant, what can I do to
make him change, his family, his upbringing was hard.” All our attention is over there, and we're not
being with ourselves or having compassion and empathy for our own experience.
This is where we become blinded by our empathy and understanding for the other person, and
start tolerating dynamics and behaviour we don't want, that hurt us, and that don't meet our
standards. We prioritise maintaining connection itself — not the quality of the connection or how we
feel in it — because safety equals connection. That's the underlying anxious attachment pattern,
and it's exactly why you see a lack of boundaries.
These patterns are dysfunctional, but we sometimes mistake them for personality: we see
ourselves as such a loving, caring, empathetic person, almost priding ourselves on caring and
loving so much that we let people treat us badly. That stands in the way of healthy boundaries,
because we've associated having boundaries with being mean or unloving.
Your safety in a relationship is your responsibility. Nobody is coming to set boundaries for you;
nobody can read your mind. You need to speak for yourself, or you won't get what you want. Safety
is co-created. We're so scared of speaking up because we think we'll lose the connection — so we
stay silent. We need to consider what we are experiencing, not just what they are experiencing.
Walls instead of boundaries (the avoidant end)


On the opposite end are people who bring the focus only onto their own experience: “I feel upset by
this, so I need to cut you off — you're a bad person.” They're quick to set extreme boundaries —
concrete walls that push people away, rather than boundaries that inform the dynamic and tell the
other person how you want to be related to. This isn't heartlessness; it's protective.
Pushing people away too quickly is often a way of avoiding closeness, discomfort, conflict, difficult
conversations, and being seen. You often see this in people with an avoidant attachment style:
there's such a lack of safety in connection, and conflict isn't experienced as safe in any capacity, so
they avoid it.
This is problematic because it comes with a tendency to be quite judgemental — with ourselves
and others — and to easily project our fears. We have to find the centre between the two ends.
