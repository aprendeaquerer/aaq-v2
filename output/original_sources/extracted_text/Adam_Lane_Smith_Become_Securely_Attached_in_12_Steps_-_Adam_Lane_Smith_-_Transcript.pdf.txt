You Can Become Securely Attached in 12 Steps —
 Adam Lane Smith
Why attachment can change
You can become securely attached in 12 steps, but they have to happen in this specific order. If
you're still anxious, avoidant, or disorganized despite everything you've done, the missing steps —
and where you've been getting stuck — are here. Adam Lane Smith, attachment specialist, has
worked with thousands of anxious, avoidant, and disorganized clients and watched them rebuild
secure attachment with these exact steps.
Most people arrive convinced they cannot change, but the science says otherwise. Attachment is a
pattern your brain built in infancy to survive with what it had. The brain is neuroplastic — moldable
— including the limbic system (the fear-based survival programming: fight, flight, fawn, freeze).
About 60 days of correct reps in the correct behavioral pathways and the brain can start changing.
The 12 steps are the sequence for reformatting that limbic response, rerouting around the
prefrontal cortex. Skip even one and the rest may stop working. Attachment is a pattern your brain
runs, not who you are.
Steps 1–3: the hardest, and they come first
The behaviors that keep you stuck are the same ones that kept you safe as a child: chasing people
who pull away, shutting down when things get too close, testing others to see if they'll fail you,
fawning and people-pleasing to make discomfort go away. Letting them go feels like ruining your
life, but it's the only way to get it back.
Step 1 — Realize your current relationship system is defensive, not relational. You're reacting to
everyone, not relating to them. Every move is a defense against a perceived childhood fear; the
chasing, people-pleasing, shutting down, and testing feel like love but are the same old loop. This
step means admitting “I've been trying to stay safe instead of trying to be happy.” Without it, the
next 11 don't work.
Step 2 — Stop chasing emotional intensity as your proof of love. The roller coaster of intensity isn't
love; it's your nervous system reacting to biochemistry, seeking safety instead of happiness.
Anxious people chase the post-fight high of dopamine and oxytocin (“I still love you”). Avoidant
people chase novelty dopamine, then drift or flee when closeness gets too high. Disorganized
people swing between both. Calm is what secure bonding feels like — mistaking intensity for love
is a cortisol addiction.
Step 3 — Replace fantasy-based love with reality-based love. Stop fixating on who the other
person could become if you loved them enough, or replaying their best version from years ago.
Look at your behaviors and theirs right now: consistency, honesty, repair, reliability, transparency,
follow-through. Is this person showing up the way they say they will? Reality-based love is
sustainable, not magical.
Steps 4–7: stop defensive behaviors, start relational ones


Step 4 — Stop the secret testing and hidden contracts. For example, testing your partner to see if
he'll detect a need you never said out loud (“if he loved me, he would attune to my feelings”). These
feel protective but keep you alone and your relationships unstable. Flip it: ask directly, say the thing
you need out loud, and see how they respond. Explicit communication of exact, measurable needs
is the heart of sustainable relationships.
Step 5 — Tolerate closeness and vulnerability without escaping. Anxious people escape into
clinging and merging; avoidant people escape into work, hobbies, and distance; both escape into
fawning when it gets too close. Instead, sit with the discomfort for 30 seconds longer than you
normally would. Discomfort is what change feels like at first — don't escape it.
Step 6 — Build safety through actions, not performance. Performing is doing things to be seen as
good; acting from safety is doing what you mean and showing who you actually are. Safety is built
on following through on declarations — showing up when you said you would, and repairing even
when you've fallen short.
Step 7 — Replace guessing with check-ins. Instead of “they must hate me,” say “I want to check in
— are we okay, or is there something we need to fix?” Instead of “she's pulling away,” ask “are you
looking for some distance right now?” Most of what you guess is wrong, and most of what you
avoid is fixable in 90 seconds — yet you ruminate for days. By step 7, the new behaviors start
happening on their own (a “flight toward health”).
Steps 8–10: build the behaviors of secure attachment
Step 8 — Learn coregulation instead of self-protection. Self-protection is isolating, clinging,
performing, or shutting down. Coregulation is calming with someone and them calming with you: a
hand on your back, breathing together, a 90-second check-in, a friendly pat, something joyful. The
human nervous system wasn't meant to regulate alone — it forms and is altered in relation with
other humans.
Step 9 — Develop conflict repair instead of conflict avoidance. Most insecurely attached people
fear one fight will end everything, so they explode, flee, fawn, or freeze. Repair is the correct skill:
the fight happens, then you come back, name what went wrong, apologize for your part, make a
better plan together, and implement it. Done a few times, conflict starts to feel like bonding and
trust.
Step 10 — Rewire your nervous system through repeated safe experiences. Once you've done the
steps, do the reps: honesty, reassurance, stability, responsiveness, accessibility — a hundred
small moments where the danger your amygdala expected never came. Insight gets you to step 1;
reps get you all the way there.
Steps 11–12: security as a way of life
Step 11 — Expand security beyond romance. Most start with romance because it hurts the most,
but secure attachment applies to friendships, family, children, co-workers, leadership, and
community. Apply the same 12 steps everywhere, and your brain stops treating positive
experiences as a fluke and starts treating them as the truth everywhere.
Step 12 — Create a lifestyle with no emotional guessing. Clarity becomes your baseline: you know
where you stand, you say what you mean, repair is fast and expected, communication is calm.
People stop having to guess where they stand with you; you stop scanning for danger and stop


running from it. That is what peace feels like.
What secure attachment feels like — and your first move
Becoming securely attached (a “remade secure style”) doesn't feel like the clouds parting. It feels
like waking up one morning and not replaying last night's conversation for the fourth time, not
pre-composing an apology before anything is wrong, not counting the hours since they last
messaged. It's the same you, free from the defenses you built as a child — and it changes how you
show up in marriage, with your children, at work, and with friends.
Your first move this week: pick one relationship and watch yourself in it for three days. Notice
where you're reacting and defending instead of relating, and write down two or three of those
moments. Don't change anything yet — just observe, honestly and specifically. That's step 1. Step
2 starts the next morning. You don't need all 12 steps today; you need step 1 this week. The rest
follows.
