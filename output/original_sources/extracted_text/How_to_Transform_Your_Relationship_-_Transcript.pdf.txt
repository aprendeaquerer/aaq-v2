How to Transform Your Relationship
1. Approach yourself with self compassion
The first thing to focus on if we want to completely transform the way we relate is the flavour with
which we approach our emotions, our needs, and our pain. Self compassion is the name of the
game. Before we even dive into resolving old pain or healing certain wounds, the way we approach
ourselves is the most important thing you will ever learn in this work.
This is where most people get stuck, because they never get this memo and just sit in a loop of
trying to do this work from the very wounds they're trying to heal. We constantly approach our pain
with an attitude of judgement: this needs to go away, this is the thing that makes me not good
enough, this is what's in the way of me getting what I want. When we approach ourselves —
especially the tender parts inside us — with frustration, judgement, and shame, we will not get
anywhere.
The way you deal with yourself — your emotions, your needs, your pain, the uncomfortable stuff —
becomes the blueprint for what you expect and tolerate from other people. If you treat yourself with
harshness, judgement, and criticism, it's no wonder your relationships reflect that back to you.
It's also no wonder you struggle to get your needs met, because chances are you're not good at
communicating them. If you're harsh in your internal voice about your emotions, you're probably
not good at being vulnerable. Instead of openly saying “this is what I'm feeling,” it comes out as an
attack, as shaming or blaming the other person — because you're carrying shame around your
own experience. It's easier to attack, project, or explode than to open up and let the other person
see you.
If you approach your needs with judgement — “why do I need these things, I should need less” —
it's no wonder you self-abandon: at the first sign of pushback you drop your needs and do what the
other person wants.
And if you approach everything you do with harshness, especially when you didn't do it the way
you wanted, it's no wonder you stay stuck exactly where you are. Nobody has ever shamed
themselves out of a pattern. Every pattern is a protective mechanism — you do it for a reason —
and shaming yourself for it doesn't create safety, so it doesn't allow your system to open,
transform, and find a new way. If you constantly tell yourself you're not enough, it's no wonder you
tolerate people who make you feel more of that. Start practising approaching yourself with curiosity
and compassion.
2. Accept fear and discomfort as part of the process
Second, we need to start accepting fear and discomfort as part of the process. It's meant to feel
scary sometimes. It's not about soothing the pain away or getting rid of it — it's about telling
yourself: it's okay that this is here, it's okay that this hurts. This is really uncomfortable, and of
course I'd feel this way; I'm going to feel it until it's done. It's part of the process. Building the
capacity for discomfort is incredibly important.
In today's mental health culture there's almost a silent expectation that we should reach a place
where nothing hurts — that if something is uncomfortable, it's always something to fix, a sign
something is going wrong or that I'm doing something wrong and need to change so I'm not in pain


anymore.
A common question is: how can I be totally detached in dating and not care at all if a connection
doesn't work out, or if my partner does XYZ? That's not how it works — that's not life. The idea that
everything should go the way we want, and only then are we okay and fully healed, is never going
to happen. A life that feels 100% comfortable is a fantasy, and a dangerous one, because it keeps
you constantly questioning yourself, your relationship, and expecting perfection.
It's normal to be disappointed if someone ghosts you. That doesn't mean you're broken or that
there's a wound you have to heal. That's life — it's an ouch. The expectation that we'd completely
get rid of certain discomfort isn't real.
3. Look in the mirror
Third, we need to look in the mirror when it comes to relationships. The problem is not out there,
not in them, not in what they're doing or not doing — it's right here. That's hard, because obsessing
about them gives us a sense of control: we try to figure out what's wrong with them, why this
always happens, why we always end up feeling this way, what they're doing wrong.
Why do we do that? So we can avoid the discomfort of having to do things differently ourselves.
Instead, we need to understand: how am I contributing to the dynamic I'm experiencing now?
Those moments of looking in the mirror show us why we stay stuck with people who don't show up
for us the way we want.
Whenever you're obsessed with them — what they're doing, how, and why — you're out of your
depth, because you're never going to change them. Being obsessed with them actually distracts
you from the impact their behaviour has on you, because you don't want to face the really
uncomfortable thing: doing something different, communicating better, walking away, or setting a
boundary. If you want to change your ways, expect it to be hard.
Practise looking at how you relate to yourself, your discomfort, and your pain. Soften that voice.
Always think about little you.
4. Get to know yourself and your values
The next step is to actually spend time getting to know yourself and your values. This matters
because your life will only be as fulfilling as it is aligned with your personal values. Your values are
completely yours — they're individual — and it's up to you to figure out what they are, so you can
take action that creates a life aligned with them.
It's also an important part of finding a person who is truly compatible with you. If you don't really
know yourself, you'll have a hard time picking the right person.
