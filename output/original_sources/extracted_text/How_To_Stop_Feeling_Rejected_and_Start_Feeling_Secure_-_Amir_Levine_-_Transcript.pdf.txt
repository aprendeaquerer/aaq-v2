How To Stop Feeling Rejected & Start Feeling Secure
 — Dr. Amir Levine
Source: How To Stop Feeling Rejected & Start Feeling Secure — Dr. Amir Levine — YouTube
What attachment theory is
Attachment theory is a basic science of how we behave in close relationships. Before I learned it,
dating and managing relationships felt like the blind leading the blind. I came across it by chance
during my child psychiatry residency: I knew about attachment in children, but didn't know that
adults attach in very similar ways, with the same three styles. I was going through a breakup at the
time, and it was so helpful — I felt like I was seeing the relationship world in colour for the first time.
Instead of endlessly complaining about an ex (which makes people sick of you), my high-school
best friend Rachel and I channelled that energy into writing Attached. It came out more than 15
years ago and is now published in about 42 languages — which is wild, because we wrote it for
ourselves and never expected it to go anywhere.
Marrying attachment science and neuroscience
My new book, Secure, is about the marriage between attachment science and neuroscience — my
knowledge as a therapist combined with sitting in the lab analysing data on how the brain works.
When I came across attachment theory it clicked into place, but it isn't fully a science yet. I wanted
to bring the lab in to answer how people can actually become more secure.
We have different attachment styles, which we can lump into two groups: secure and insecure.
There are evolutionary advantages to insecure styles, but for your own personal life, it's much
better to enter what I call secure mode — and we can all become more secure. That's the
surprising part of this science.
Attachment styles are not fixed attributes. The science has advanced: we no longer think in rigid
categories but in terms of a spectrum, and we can have a different attachment style with different
people. In the book and on my website there's a questionnaire that maps your “attachment
topography” — your style with your best friend, your partner, even your pet — which you then use
as a chart to become more secure.
Pets as a model of security
Attachment is about how comfortable we feel with closeness and how sensitive we are to potential
danger in a relationship. Even pets vary: my dog likes to be close but from a distance, at the edge
of the bed, and doesn't like to be petted much. A cat that's extremely affectionate, loves closeness,
with zero ambiguity about its affection — that's exactly what secure is. Zero ambiguity about
closeness: bring it on, she'll take it.
Most of us are more secure with our pets, so I use that relationship as a model. The idea in
attachment science is that we all have secure kernels inside us — “angels in the nursery,” the good
people and good things that left an imprint. Therapy often emphasises difficult relationships (the
“ghosts in the nursery”), but in my secure priming therapy we deliberately talk about the secure
people in our lives, past and present.


Childhood doesn't determine your adult style
Importantly, the attachment style you have as a child is not necessarily the one you have as an
adult — less than 10% (somewhere between 4 and 9%) of childhood attachment explains adult
attachment. That's promising. Bowlby called attachment a “working model”: a set of beliefs about
the world that the brain constantly checks and tries to update. But the brain isn't a neutral observer
— it notices things that fit the belief and filters out what doesn't. Secure priming teaches people to
shift their gaze toward a more secure belief and see what's already there.
You don't get to choose your childhood, but you get to edit a lot of your experiences and introduce
new ones, morphing your attachment style.
The Cyberball effect: how the brain hates exclusion
Here's what your brain hates: exclusion. I call it the Cyberball effect, after experiments with a
simple video game of catch. When the two other players stop throwing you the ball, brain areas of
painful distress and self-scrutiny light up, and people report lower self-esteem, less agency, and
that life feels less meaningful — all from something tiny. Even being told the other players are
members of the KKK, or being paid money each time you're excluded, doesn't mitigate the effect.
The brain is that sensitive.
Exclusion shows up everywhere: seeing on Instagram that friends had a dinner or trip without you,
someone not liking your post. Our social brain constantly scans for exclusion. Research links it on
par with physical pain — and our culture, which no longer accepts physical violence, still tolerates
exclusion.
The opposite is reverse Cyberball — hyper-inclusion, where the ball always comes through you
and nobody is excluded. The brain loves it: more self-esteem, more agency, more control, plus real
health benefits. Securely attached people are healthier across many domains: better management
of illnesses like fibromyalgia, fewer symptoms, better longevity and cognition. The social impact on
health is as strong as exercise and diet. (If you're going to McDonald's, take a trusted person to
offset some of the effect.)
Why exclusion hurts so much (evolution)
Why is exclusion so powerful? This part of our emotional brain formed when we were in the middle
of the food chain, not the top. On a safari, guides made us walk in single file with no gaps — “close
the gap!” — because even a momentary lapse in staying together could once mean being picked
off by a predator. Our brain still interprets exclusion as not being safe, which triggers inflammation,
stress, and other harmful effects.
The five pillars: CARP
To change the brain toward security, you need to be immersed in a secure environment — like an
“enriched environment” in animal research. Intermittent rewards (the way social media sometimes
makes you feel hyper-included, like playing slots in Vegas) won't cut it; consistency is key.
I use the five pillars of a secure life, with the acronym CARP: Consistent, Available, Responsive,
Predictable — and it must be relational. It's not enough to think you're great at it; you have to reach
out so the other person actually experiences you as reliable and predictable. Be CARP yourself,
and teach others to be CARP with you, and you create a secure bubble — an enriched


environment with profound effects, even on how you relate to yourself (more secure people are
even less affected by consumerism).
This applies far beyond romance — friendships, work, children, even retirement homes (exclusion
happens there too). A big message of the book is giving you tools to curate your social
environment and enter secure mode.
The CARP intervention
When you keep feeling the Cyberball effect in a relationship, it's time for a CARP intervention. You
tell the person something like: “I've learned this concept about living in secure mode — being
consistent, available, responsive. It's important to me, I'm going to try to be this way, and I'd like
others to be that way with me too.” It's an invitation for the other person to summon their own
secure kernel. Sometimes it works beautifully.
If they can't rise to it, remember that most of us already have secure people who are automatically
CARP — but we ignore them because there's no drama; they're “boring,” so the brain doesn't fixate
on them. The insecure relationships create excitement, disappointment, and drama that divert our
attention. The work is to shift your gaze to the people who always text back, always show up, are
always there.
Make sure you're not the one excluding others, either. Attention is one of the rarest forms of
generosity — bestowing it on others is giving something very valuable, and it helps everyone feel
connected. Society tolerates exclusion (“I don't owe them an invitation”), but shifting that mindset
creates a secure space for you and others. It's less work than people think.
Micro interactions and the secure practice
It comes down to micro interactions — the seemingly insignificant minor moments of everyday life
we don't pay attention to, which are actually crucial for changing the brain. When people go to
therapy, they think they must dig into childhood and big events, and rarely consider small
day-to-day interactions as a vehicle for change.
From an epigenetic perspective, the brain is extremely sensitive to its environment. If you
remember anything from a conversation tomorrow, some histone acetylation has happened — tags
added to DNA, changes in the synapse, even the same proteins (like actin) that build the
scaffolding of synapses. Our interactions can change gene expression and synaptic architecture.
So saying hi to the barista, nodding to someone at the gym, texting a friend who pops into your
head — these accumulate like a piggy bank, and the brain loves it. I call it a secure practice: not
going out of your way, just interacting with secure people who are CARP. It's not much work; it's
fun and good. You can even apply it to frustrating calls with an airline — the person on the line isn't
at fault, so you can make even that interaction more secure.
Emotion regulation and the attachment hierarchy
One of the most powerful ways humans regulate emotions is through other people — and that
transcends childhood. Mary Ainsworth discovered the three styles in the Strange Situation test: a
toddler plays happily with toys, the caregiver leaves and the child becomes distressed, and at the
reunion you see the style. Secure children calm down quickly when picked up and return to play.
Anxious children take a long time to console. Avoidant children may not cry and stay limp — but


their blood pressure is through the roof; they're just not good at using the caregiver to regulate
emotions.
We each have an attachment hierarchy — our brain knows exactly who to go to first when
something bad happens. (Even your cat has one, and you're number one.)
Wall tennis with love
When a CARP intervention doesn't land, don't cut the person off — do “wall tennis with love.” Like
hitting a tennis ball against a wall: whatever they dish, you return with a little less oomph, same
direction. When they reach out, you respond with love and affection, but you don't initiate as much.
You deprioritise the relationship — they no longer board in group one of your social life, maybe
group eight — while engaging more with secure people.
This isn't passive aggression. You can only get so much water from a stone; if someone is capable
of only what they're capable of, demanding more isn't fair to them. With one friend I do this, and
instead of making the relationship disappear, it improved it. It also breaks the “protest–regret cycle”
— getting upset, lashing out, feeling bad, apologising, then repeating.
It's like a cat: if you pick her up and put her on the couch, she'll leap off, but if it's her idea, she'll
come. You leave the door open for people to come as much as they want, with love, without
dragging them. This also helps parents of adolescents, who naturally become more avoidant as
they individuate — latching on harder creates strife; love without force lets them come to you.
The secure mind, hidden talents and brain energy
The last part of the book is about the secure mind — reshaping insecure beliefs to create more
security. From a neuroscientist's view, we're each molecularly diverse, so we each have hidden
sparks of talent. People with insecure attachment often have such sparks but don't know they're
there, or perceive them as impediments (“too sensitive”), instead of understanding they just need
the right environment. I cite the novel Convenience Store Woman, about a neurodivergent woman
whose biology feels good working in a convenience store despite social pressure — the book
teaches being more true to your biology.
Energy in the brain matters too. The brain weighs 2% of the body but uses 10–20% of its energy,
and it can't recruit more — it can only move energy around. When you're not in fight-or-flight (“what
are they doing to me?”), energy frees up for creativity — just like the child who plays once it feels
safe. We all need safety moments to open that gate.
Why do some people energise us and others drain us? There's “collective effervescence” and
interpersonal synchrony — when you click with someone, there's a surge of energy and you can do
more. In one study, people judged a mountain's slope as less steep when they'd climb it with a
trusted person. If the people you feel closest to are the ones draining you, while the energising
ones are far down your list, that's an invitation to subtly shift your priorities — not to cut people off,
but to invest more in the relationships that are genuine assets to your brain.
Misconceptions and becoming secure
The biggest misconceptions, all over social media: that attachment styles are a diagnosis,
something to heal from, something fixed that comes only from childhood. All wrong — they're
changeable, and we don't have one single style but an attachment topography. You can even be


secure in one relationship at one point and more insecure at another.
In my practice I try to build a secure village, helping people find secure relationships. At first secure
relationships feel uncomfortable and unfamiliar — “is this okay?” — because people are used to
drama. The work is to say: hang in there, ease into it, and you'll never look back. The environment
then catapults them and revises their world beliefs: people aren't going to leave you or surprise you
in a bad way.
On psychedelic-assisted therapy and ketamine: I find them interesting and use secure priming
alongside them. They're especially helpful for summoning those secure kernels — people
sometimes remember or feel that connection in a psychedelic journey and see what's possible,
which gives motivation to take the next steps in everyday life. It gives you a window into what's
possible.
Owning the work
When Attached came out, I was actually embarrassed — I thought a serious neuroscientist should
focus on “serious” work, and a then-insecure partner reinforced that. Later a woman asked why I
was presenting neurobiology when I'd written something far more important, and I realised: it's not
only medications that change the brain — ideas change the brain in a huge way (think how
concepts like “all are created equal” reshaped how we live). That's when I owned my inner spark of
talent and wrote Secure.
A moving email from a woman in Iran thanked us: the book helped her express herself more
securely, find a new partner, and articulate her needs. Ideas, like meditation, don't change the facts
— they change the narrative.
Secure priming is about appreciating the people who propel us forward and give us energy and
encouragement — and recognising how hard it is when we don't have them, or when people feed
us the wrong ideas.
